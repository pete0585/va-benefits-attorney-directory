# Preparing for Your First VA Benefits Attorney Consultation

If you're a veteran, a family member, or someone supporting a veteran, you may be considering whether to speak with a VA benefits attorney. The first consultation is often where you decide whether the relationship is a good fit and whether the attorney can help with your situation. A little preparation can make that conversation more productive and less stressful.

This guide is not legal advice. It is a practical checklist and a set of questions to discuss with a qualified professional. Every situation is different, and only a qualified professional can advise you on your specific circumstances. You should discuss your situation with a qualified professional before making decisions.

## Why preparation matters

A first consultation is usually a two-way conversation. You are learning about the attorney's experience and approach, and the attorney is learning about your situation. When you arrive organized, you can spend more time on the substance of your questions and less time trying to recall dates, documents, or details. Preparation also helps you notice whether the attorney's communication style and focus match what you need.

## What to gather before the consultation

You do not need a perfect file. You need enough information to have a useful conversation. Consider assembling the following, if you have it. If you do not have something, that is okay; note it as a gap to discuss.

- **Basic identifying information**: your name, contact information, and the best way to reach you.
- **Military service details**: branch, dates of service, and any periods of active duty. If you have a DD-214 or similar separation document, have it available.
- **VA correspondence**: any letters, decision notices, or statements you have received from the VA. These can include decisions on claims, requests for more information, or notices about deadlines.
- **Claim history**: a short timeline of what you have filed, when, and what happened. You do not need to write a book; a bullet list is fine.
- **Medical and treatment records**: if relevant to your situation, gather records you already have. Do not request new records solely for the consultation unless the attorney asks you to.
- **A list of your questions**: write them down so you do not forget them in the moment.
- **Notes on your goals**: what outcome are you hoping for? A new claim, an appeal, a higher rating, or simply understanding your options?
- **A way to take notes**: a notebook, a note-taking app, or a trusted person who can take notes for you.

## Questions to ask about the attorney's background and approach

The following questions are designed to help you understand whether the attorney is a good fit for your needs. You can ask them in any order, and you can ask follow-up questions.

- What is your experience with VA benefits matters similar to mine?
- Are you accredited to represent veterans before the VA? You can ask how they are accredited and what that means for your case.
- How do you typically communicate with clients? How often should I expect updates?
- Who will be handling my case day to day? Will I be working with you or with someone else in your office?
- What is your approach to cases like mine? Do you prefer to resolve matters informally, or do you often proceed to formal appeals?
- How do you decide whether to take a case?
- What are the potential risks or challenges in a situation like mine?
- What alternatives might I consider, and what are the trade-offs?
- How do you handle deadlines and time-sensitive filings?
- What should I do if I have a question between meetings?
- How do you keep clients informed about changes in the law or process that might affect them?

## Questions to ask about process, costs, and expectations

Costs and fees are a common source of uncertainty. You can ask direct questions about how the attorney charges and what you should expect. The answers may depend on the type of matter and the stage of your case.

- How are your fees structured for a matter like mine? For example, is it an hourly fee, a flat fee, or a contingency fee?
- What costs might I be responsible for beyond your fee? For example, are there filing fees, records fees, or other expenses?
- When would I need to pay, and what payment options are available?
- What is the typical timeline for a matter like mine? What milestones should I expect?
- What information do you need from me to get started?
- What are the possible outcomes, and what happens if the outcome is not what I hope for?
- Are there any alternatives to working with an attorney that I should consider?
- What would you do if you were in my situation?

## Questions to ask about evidence and your role

Evidence is often central to VA benefits matters. You can ask how the attorney thinks about evidence and what your role will be in gathering it.

- What kinds of evidence are typically important in a matter like mine?
- What evidence do I already have that might be useful?
- What evidence might I need to obtain, and how would I go about getting it?
- How do you evaluate the strength of evidence?
- What role will I play in gathering information or records?
- How do you handle situations where records are incomplete or missing?
- What should I do if I remember new information after our meeting?

## A short note on accreditation and official resources

In the United States, individuals who represent veterans before the VA are generally required to be accredited. The VA provides an official resource explaining accreditation and how to find a VA-accredited representative: [VA Accredited Representative FAQs](https://www.va.gov/resources/va-accredited-representative-faqs/). You can use this resource to understand the basics and to check accreditation status. This article does not claim to have reviewed that page; it is provided as a starting point for your own review.

## What to do after the consultation

After the meeting, take a few minutes to review your notes while the conversation is fresh. Consider the following:

- Did the attorney answer your questions clearly?
- Did you feel comfortable with the communication style?
- Did you understand the next steps and any deadlines?
- Did you receive a clear explanation of fees and costs?
- Do you have a sense of whether this attorney is a good fit for your needs?

If you are unsure, it is reasonable to speak with more than one attorney before deciding. You can also ask the attorney for clarification on anything you did not understand.

## Common concerns and how to address them

- **I don't have all my records.** That is common. Ask the attorney what they need first and how to prioritize.
- **I'm not sure my situation is serious enough.** You can ask the attorney whether your situation is one they handle and what options might exist.
- **I'm worried about cost.** Ask directly about fees and costs, and ask whether there are alternatives or payment options.
- **I'm not sure I want to pursue a claim.** Ask about the process, timelines, and what happens if you decide not to proceed.
- **I had a bad experience before.** You can share that with the attorney and ask how they would approach your situation differently.

## A practical checklist you can print or copy

Before your consultation:

- [ ] Gather service documents (e.g., DD-214) if available.
- [ ] Collect VA letters and decision notices.
- [ ] Write a short timeline of your claim history.
- [ ] List your questions.
- [ ] Note your goals for the consultation.
- [ ] Prepare a way to take notes.

During your consultation:

- [ ] Ask about the attorney's experience and accreditation.
- [ ] Ask about communication and who handles your case.
- [ ] Ask about fees, costs, and payment timing.
- [ ] Ask about timelines and next steps.
- [ ] Ask about evidence and your role.
- [ ] Ask about risks, alternatives, and what happens if the outcome is not favorable.

After your consultation:

- [ ] Review your notes.
- [ ] Write down any follow-up questions.
- [ ] Decide whether you need more information or another consultation.
- [ ] If you proceed, confirm next steps and deadlines in writing if possible.
