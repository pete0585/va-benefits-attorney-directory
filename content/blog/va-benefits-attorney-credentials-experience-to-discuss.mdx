## Why credentials and experience deserve a real conversation

Choosing a VA benefits attorney is not a single decision. It is a series of smaller decisions: what kind of help you need, which experience areas matter for your situation, what credentials you should ask about, and how you prefer to work with someone over months or years.

This guide is written for people researching VA benefits attorney services. It focuses on what to discuss during provider selection. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a consultation with a qualified professional. Licensing rules, accreditation, and fee arrangements can vary, so confirm the specifics with the provider and, where relevant, with the appropriate oversight body.

A useful way to approach this is to separate **credentials** from **experience**, then decide which combination fits your situation.

- **Credentials** are the formal markers: licensing, accreditation, standing with relevant bodies, and any specialized training or certifications the provider says they hold.
- **Experience** is the practical pattern: the types of claims, appeals, or issues the provider handles regularly, and how they describe their process.

Both matter, and neither replaces the other.

## Start with your own situation, not the provider list

Before comparing providers, write down a short summary of your situation. This helps you ask better questions and compare answers across providers.

Consider:

- **What stage are you at?** Initial claim, supplemental claim, higher-level review, appeal, or a question about an existing decision.
- **What is the main issue?** Service connection, rating level, effective date, dependency or survivor issues, or something else.
- **What is your timeline?** Are there deadlines or review windows you are worried about?
- **What do you want from a provider?** Full representation, a second opinion, help organizing records, or guidance on next steps.

You do not need to know the legal labels. A short written summary is enough to make the conversation more productive.

## Credentials worth discussing

Credentials are not a single checkbox. Ask about each of the following and write down the answers.

### 1. Authority to represent you

Ask how the provider is authorized to represent veterans before the relevant agency or board. The specifics can change, so ask the provider to explain their current authorization and how it applies to your type of matter. If you are unsure, ask where you can verify it.

### 2. Licensing and standing

For attorneys, ask about bar membership and whether there are any restrictions or disciplinary matters you should know about. Many jurisdictions allow public lookup of licensing status. If the provider is not an attorney, ask what type of accredited representative they are and who oversees that accreditation.

### 3. Relevant training or specialization

Ask whether the provider has any training, certifications, or focused practice areas relevant to your issue. Treat these as claims to verify, not as guarantees of outcome. A certification is not the same as a result.

### 4. Continuing education and current practice

Ask how the provider stays current on changes that affect claims and appeals. You are looking for a habit of updating knowledge, not a specific answer.

### 5. Team structure

Ask who will actually work on your matter. Will you speak with the attorney or representative directly, or with a team member? Who reviews the work before it is filed? This affects communication and accountability.

## Experience areas worth discussing

Experience is where you learn whether a provider's day-to-day work matches your needs. Ask for patterns, not promises.

### 1. Types of matters handled

Ask what share of their work involves the kind of issue you have. For example, if your question is about a rating decision, ask how often they handle rating-related matters. If your question is about an appeal, ask how often they handle appeals at the relevant stage.

### 2. Stage-of-claim experience

Initial claims, supplemental claims, higher-level reviews, and appeals can involve different skills and timelines. Ask which stages the provider handles most often and whether they handle the stage you are in.

### 3. Record development

Ask how they approach gathering and organizing evidence. Who identifies what is missing? Who follows up? How do they keep you informed about what they need from you?

### 4. Communication and response expectations

Ask how they typically communicate, how often you should expect updates, and who to contact with questions. Ask what happens if your point of contact changes.

### 5. Transitions and handoffs

Ask what happens if the provider cannot continue, if your matter changes direction, or if you decide to change representation. A clear answer here is a good sign of an organized practice.

## A practical checklist you can use

Use this checklist during or after each conversation. It is designed to help you compare providers on the same dimensions.

**Authority and licensing**
- [ ] How are you authorized to represent veterans in my type of matter?
- [ ] Where can I verify your licensing or accreditation?
- [ ] Are there any restrictions or disciplinary matters I should know about?

**Experience fit**
- [ ] What share of your work involves my type of issue?
- [ ] Which stages of claims or appeals do you handle most often?
- [ ] Can you describe your typical process from start to finish?

**Team and communication**
- [ ] Who will work on my matter day to day?
- [ ] How and how often will you communicate with me?
- [ ] Who do I contact if I have a question?

**Records and evidence**
- [ ] How do you identify what evidence is missing?
- [ ] What do you need from me, and by when?
- [ ] How do you keep track of deadlines?

**Fees and agreements**
- [ ] How are fees structured for my type of matter?
- [ ] What is included, and what is not?
- [ ] What should I expect in writing before we begin?

**Fit and comfort**
- [ ] Did the provider answer my questions directly?
- [ ] Did they explain things in a way I understood?
- [ ] Did they avoid promising a specific outcome?

If a provider is vague on authority, licensing, or fees, treat that as a reason to ask follow-up questions before moving forward.

## Clearly labeled hypothetical examples

The following examples are hypothetical and are included only to show how the checklist can be used. They are not real cases, not success stories, and not predictions.

**Example 1: A veteran with a pending rating decision.**
A veteran has a rating decision they do not understand and wants to know whether to file a supplemental claim or request a higher-level review. Using the checklist, they ask each provider which stage they handle most often, how they develop evidence, and how they explain timelines. One provider describes a structured intake and a written summary of next steps. Another gives general answers without specifics. The veteran uses the checklist to compare, not to predict an outcome.

**Example 2: A veteran considering a change in representation.**
A veteran is already working with a representative but wants a second opinion on how their appeal is being handled. Using the checklist, they ask about authority, how the provider would review existing materials, and what a transition would look like. The provider explains what they would need and what they would not change without the veteran's agreement. The veteran notes the answers and decides whether to proceed.

**Example 3: A survivor with questions about dependency and indemnity compensation.**
A survivor is researching providers and is unsure which experience areas matter. Using the checklist, they ask whether the provider handles survivor matters, how they communicate, and what records they typically need. The provider explains their process and notes where they would consult with the survivor before filing anything.

In each example, the point is the same: ask about credentials and experience, write down the answers, and compare providers on the same dimensions.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Treating a credential as a guarantee.** Credentials show authority and training, not a specific result.
- **Skipping the fee conversation.** Ask how fees work for your type of matter and get the details in writing where possible.
- **Ignoring communication style.** You may work with a provider for a long time. Ask how updates happen and who answers questions.
- **Assuming all providers handle all stages.** Ask which stages they handle most often.
- **Relying on a single conversation.** Compare at least two providers using the same checklist.
