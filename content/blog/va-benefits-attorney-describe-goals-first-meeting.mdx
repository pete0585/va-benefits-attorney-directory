# VA Benefits Attorney: How to Describe Your Goals at the First Meeting

Your first meeting with a VA benefits attorney is a working conversation, not a test. The clearer you are about what you want to accomplish, the more useful the attorney's guidance can be. This guide walks through how to identify, organize, and describe your goals so that you and the attorney can decide together whether and how to move forward.

This article offers general information for people researching VA benefits attorney services. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a consultation with a qualified professional. Individual circumstances vary, and VA processes can change. Always confirm details with a qualified attorney or accredited representative.

## Why Describing Goals Matters

A VA benefits matter can involve many possible paths: an initial claim, a supplemental claim, a higher-level review, a Board appeal, a remand, a motion to revise a prior decision, or questions about dependents and survivors. Each path has its own timing, evidence expectations, and procedural rules.

When you describe your goals clearly, you help the attorney:

- Understand what outcome matters most to you.
- Identify which procedural route may fit your situation.
- Flag deadlines or jurisdictional issues that could affect your options.
- Explain tradeoffs in plain language.
- Decide whether they can help and how they would work with you.

You do not need to know the legal terminology. You do need to be able to say what you want and why it matters.

## Start With the Outcome, Not the Process

Many people open a first meeting by describing a process problem: "My claim was denied," or "My rating hasn't changed in years." That is useful context, but it is not the same as a goal. A goal describes the result you are seeking.

Try translating your situation into an outcome statement:

- Situation: "My claim was denied last year."
- Possible goal: "I want to understand whether there is a realistic path to getting that decision reviewed, and what that would require from me."

- Situation: "I think my condition has gotten worse."
- Possible goal: "I want to know whether my current rating still reflects my situation, and what steps would be involved in asking for a review."

- Situation: "I'm helping my parent, who is a veteran."
- Possible goal: "I want to understand what benefits may be available to a surviving spouse or dependent, and what information would be needed to explore that."

These are examples only. They are hypothetical and are not meant to match any real person's circumstances.

## A Checklist for Clarifying Your Goals Before the Meeting

Use this checklist to prepare. You can write your answers in a notebook or type them into a document.

1. **What is the single most important outcome for you right now?** Examples: a decision reviewed, a rating reconsidered, a new claim filed, a survivor benefit explored, or simply understanding your options.
2. **What has already happened?** Note any decisions you have received, any claims you have filed, and any deadlines you are aware of.
3. **What have you already tried?** Include prior claims, appeals, requests for records, or conversations with VA or representatives.
4. **What documents do you have?** Decision letters, medical records, service records, correspondence, and anything else you think is relevant.
5. **What documents are you missing?** Note what you have requested but not received.
6. **What is your timeline?** Are there deadlines, upcoming medical appointments, or family circumstances that affect your urgency?
7. **What questions do you want answered?** Write them down, even if they seem basic.
8. **What would a good outcome look like six months from now?** This helps separate short-term steps from long-term goals.
9. **What are your constraints?** Consider travel, work, caregiving, health, or other factors that affect how you can participate.
10. **What do you want the attorney to explain first?** For example: the process, the likely timeline, the evidence, or the possible outcomes.

Bringing this checklist to the meeting gives you a structure and helps the attorney focus on what matters to you.

## How to Describe Your Goals in the Meeting

You can use a simple format: context, goal, question.

- **Context:** "Here is what has happened so far."
- **Goal:** "Here is what I want to accomplish."
- **Question:** "Here is what I need help understanding."

For example:

> "I received a decision two years ago that I disagreed with, but I did not appeal at the time. My goal is to understand whether there is any option left, and if so, what it would involve. My question is whether the deadline has passed and what I should do next."

This format keeps the conversation focused and gives the attorney specific information to respond to.

## Hypothetical Examples of Goal Statements

The following examples are clearly hypothetical. They are intended to show how goals can be phrased, not to describe real cases or predict outcomes.

**Example 1: Reviewing a prior decision**

> "I want to know if there is a way to have an older decision looked at again. I understand there may be time limits, and I want to know what those are and whether I have any options."

**Example 2: Exploring a new claim**

> "I have never filed a claim. I want to understand what the process looks like, what evidence I would need, and how long it might take. I am not sure whether I qualify, and I want help figuring that out."

**Example 3: Asking about a rating review**

> "My situation has changed since my last rating. I want to know whether it makes sense to ask for a review, and what information would support that request."

**Example 4: Survivor benefits**

> "I am helping a family member who is a surviving spouse of a veteran. We want to understand what benefits might be available and what documents we should gather."

**Example 5: Understanding a denial**

> "I received a denial and I do not fully understand the reason. My goal is to understand the decision and to learn what options, if any, are available to me."

Each of these statements is short, specific, and focused on an outcome. None of them requires legal jargon.

## Questions to Ask About Your Goals

After you describe your goals, ask the attorney:

- Based on what I have told you, what options do you see?
- What are the deadlines I should be aware of?
- What evidence would be most helpful?
- What would you need from me to move forward?
- What are the possible outcomes, and what are the tradeoffs?
- How would you keep me informed, and how often should I expect updates?
- Are there any questions I should be asking that I have not asked?

These questions help you evaluate whether the attorney's approach aligns with your goals. They also help you understand the practical realities of your situation.

## What Not to Do

- Do not assume you know the legal process. Ask.
- Do not hide information because you are embarrassed or think it is irrelevant. Attorneys need the full picture to give useful guidance.
- Do not rely on general information, including this article, as a substitute for individualized advice.
- Do not agree to a course of action you do not understand. Ask for clarification until you do.

## Deciding Whether to Move Forward

After the meeting, take time to reflect. Consider:

- Did the attorney listen to your goals and respond to them directly?
- Did you understand the options and tradeoffs?
- Did you feel comfortable asking questions?
- Did the attorney explain what they would do and what they would need from you?
- Did you receive clear information about next steps and timing?

If something is unclear, it is reasonable to ask follow-up questions or to seek a second opinion. Choosing a provider is a decision you make, and it is appropriate to take the time you need.
